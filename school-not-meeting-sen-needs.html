School support · Guidance for parents in England

School not meeting SEN needs? Start here.

If your school is not supporting your child with SEN, ask for a review with the special educational needs co-ordinator (SENCo). If support specified in an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP) is missing, write to the local authority too. Disagreeing with an EHCP decision is a different issue and may involve an appeal.

You do not need to arrive with a perfect file. Start with what your child is finding difficult and what would help them access school.

No EHCP: ask for a clear SEN support plan

Children can receive SEN support without an EHCP. A formal diagnosis is not a prerequisite: support should respond to your child's needs, as IPSEA explains. Professional assessments can still help identify suitable support.

For mainstream state schools, including academies, the governing body or proprietor must use its “best endeavours” to secure the special educational provision needed. This means doing everything that could reasonably be expected, not simply waiting for a diagnosis or assessment. Section 66's duty does not apply to every school type; special and independent schools have a different legal position.

Ask the teacher and SENCo to follow Assess, Plan, Do, Review: identify needs, agree support, deliver it, then check whether it helps. Ask who will do what, how often, and when you will review it together.

If your child may need provision through an EHCP, you can request an EHC needs assessment yourself. School agreement is not required. Requesting assessment does not guarantee a plan.

Make the difficulty specific

Keep a short dated record of missed support, distress, attendance difficulties or work your child cannot access. Include your child's own words where possible. Note what has helped, too. A child appearing settled in class may still be struggling elsewhere.

Before meeting, read the school's SEN information report and gather the current support plan, relevant reports and recent emails. Choose two or three priorities rather than trying to resolve everything at once. For example:

“My child is struggling to understand classroom instructions. Please arrange a SEN support review. Could we agree the support to be provided, who is responsible, and a review date? Please send me a written summary afterwards.”

Confirm agreed actions by email. If the school's account differs from yours, ask what evidence it used and how your child's views will inform the plan.

EHCP in place: check missing Section F provision

Section F sets out special educational provision. Compare its actual wording with what your child receives: frequency, duration, staffing and specialist input. Keep a record of gaps, but do not delay raising known missing provision while collecting evidence.

Under section 42(2) of the Children and Families Act 2014, the local authority maintaining the plan must secure the specified special educational provision. The school may deliver it, but the authority cannot simply pass responsibility to the school. The law includes an exception where parents or the young person have made suitable alternative arrangements.

Write to the authority's SEND team, copying the SENCo. Identify the Section F wording, what is missing and since when. Ask how and when delivery will be restored. IPSEA's template letter 6 can help. You do not need to wait for the annual review to raise missing provision.

If Section F itself is vague or insufficient, changing the plan is a separate task. An annual review may help, but it does not remove the authority's duty to secure existing provision.

Complaints and EHCP appeals do different jobs

School SEN support concerns: after speaking to the SENCo, use the school's published complaints procedure if matters remain unresolved. Follow its stages, which may involve the headteacher, governors or academy trust. GOV.UK explains the school complaint route.

Missing Section F provision: raise it with the authority and consider its complaints process. Serious or urgent failures may need prompt specialist legal advice about enforcement rather than a lengthy complaint.

Disagreement with an EHCP decision: a refusal to assess or issue a plan, or disagreement about needs, provision or placement, may carry a SEND Tribunal appeal right. See our EHCP refusal and appeal guidance. An appeal challenges a decision; it is not the usual route for enforcing provision already specified.

Check deadlines immediately. The usual appeal deadline is two months from the decision letter or one month from the mediation certificate, whichever is later. Complaining does not pause it. In most cases you must contact a mediation adviser and obtain a certificate, but attending mediation is optional. Section I-only placement appeals are an exception.
