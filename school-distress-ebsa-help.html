EBSA describes a difficulty, not its whole cause

Emotionally based school avoidance (EBSA) describes attendance difficulties associated with emotional distress. It is not a diagnosis or an explanation for every absence. Barriers may include unmet SEND needs, sensory demands, bullying, health difficulties or several interacting factors.

If you searched for school refusal help in England, you may encounter older language that suggests deliberate defiance. Start by understanding what your child cannot manage rather than assuming they are choosing not to attend. Family Action explains the terminology; Not Fine in School describes wider attendance barriers.

Prepare the school meeting about EBSA

Keep short, dated observations: what happened before school, which lesson or transition was difficult, what happened afterwards and what helped. Separate what you observed from what you think caused it. Include your child's words, drawings or preferred way of communicating, without repeatedly pressing them to explain.

Contact the SENCo and attendance or pastoral lead. Share two or three priorities and ask for a meeting. Useful questions include:

  • Which barriers will we address, and how will my child's views shape the support?
  • What changes and access to learning can be arranged while attendance is difficult?
  • Who owns each action, how will we check its effect, and when will we review together?

Ask for a written action summary. Keep reporting absences through the school's procedure and explain the reasons; do not wait for a consultancy appointment to raise urgent concerns.

Fictional agenda example

Fictional mini-example: an illustrative preparation output, not a real family, clinical recommendation or promised outcome.

Barrier and child voice
A child describes the busy entrance as “too loud” and struggles to enter on assembly mornings.
Evidence to bring
Two dated parent observations, the current support plan and the child's description of a quieter arrival that felt easier.
Meeting agenda
Discuss a quieter entrance and trusted adult; ask who can arrange this and when to review whether it helps. Clarify learning access if the child still cannot attend.

Keep education and health needs in view

DfE attendance guidance supports an individual plan with parents and regular review. Any return-to-school approach should address the child's barriers; involve an appropriate clinician where health needs require it.

For compulsory-school-age children unable to attend because of health needs, statutory guidance explains local authority education duties where suitable education would otherwise be unavailable. Raise this promptly; the guidance's reference to 15 days is not an instruction to wait.

EBSA does not automatically establish entitlement to education otherwise than at school (EOTAS) through an EHCP or prevent attendance action. Seek specialist advice promptly about enforcement notices or legal deadlines.

Questions parents ask

What records help with meeting preparation?

Short dated observations, the current educational support plan and your child's own account can help. Note what was difficult and what helped, and distinguish observations from assumptions about the cause. Agree any documents for paid review before sending them.

Is this therapy or an assessment of whether my child can attend?

No. SEND Navigate offers educational consultancy and preparation for school conversations. Diagnosis, treatment and clinical fitness-to-attend assessments need an appropriate health professional.
